Bonds and Fixed Income Guide

What are Bonds?
Bonds are debt securities where an investor lends money to an entity (government or corporation) for a defined period at a fixed or variable interest rate. They're considered safer than stocks and provide regular income.

Key Bond Terms:

Face Value (Par):
- Amount paid at maturity
- Typically $1,000 per bond
- Price quoted as percentage of par

Coupon Rate:
- Annual interest rate paid
- Based on face value
- Example: 5% coupon = $50/year per $1,000 bond

Maturity Date:
- When principal is repaid
- Short-term: < 3 years
- Medium-term: 3-10 years
- Long-term: > 10 years

Yield to Maturity (YTM):
- Total return if held to maturity
- Accounts for price, coupon, and time
- Most important yield measure

Current Yield:
- Annual coupon / Current price
- Simpler but less complete measure

Types of Bonds:

Government Bonds:
Treasury Securities (US):
- T-Bills: < 1 year, no coupon
- T-Notes: 2-10 years
- T-Bonds: 20-30 years
- TIPS: Inflation-protected
- I-Bonds: Inflation-linked savings bonds

Other Government:
- Municipal Bonds: State/local government, often tax-free
- Agency Bonds: Government-sponsored enterprises
- Foreign Government: Sovereign bonds

Corporate Bonds:
- Investment Grade: BBB/Baa and above
- High Yield (Junk): Below BBB/Baa
- Convertible: Can convert to stock
- Callable: Issuer can redeem early

Other Fixed Income:
- Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)
- Asset-Backed Securities (ABS)
- Certificates of Deposit (CDs)
- Money Market Instruments

Bond Pricing:

Price and Yield Relationship:
- Inverse relationship
- When interest rates rise, bond prices fall
- When interest rates fall, bond prices rise
- Longer maturity = more price sensitivity

Premium vs Discount:
- Premium: Price > Face value (coupon > market rate)
- Par: Price = Face value
- Discount: Price < Face value (coupon < market rate)

Duration:
- Measure of interest rate sensitivity
- Higher duration = more volatile
- Longer maturity and lower coupon = higher duration
- Used to estimate price change for yield change

Credit Ratings:

Rating Agencies:
- Moody's: Aaa, Aa, A, Baa, Ba, B, Caa, Ca, C
- S&P/Fitch: AAA, AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, CC, C, D

Investment Grade:
- Moody's: Baa3 and above
- S&P: BBB- and above
- Lower default risk
- Lower yields

High Yield (Junk):
- Below investment grade
- Higher default risk
- Higher yields to compensate
- More stock-like behavior

Yield Curve:

What is the Yield Curve?
- Graph of yields across maturities
- Shows term structure of interest rates

Shapes:
- Normal: Upward sloping (longer = higher yield)
- Inverted: Downward sloping (recession indicator)
- Flat: Similar yields across maturities

Key Spreads:
- 10-Year minus 2-Year Treasury spread
- Inversion historically precedes recessions
- Credit spreads indicate risk appetite

Bond Risks:

Interest Rate Risk:
- Prices fall when rates rise
- Managed through duration matching

Credit Risk:
- Issuer may default
- Managed through diversification and ratings

Inflation Risk:
- Fixed payments lose purchasing power
- TIPS provide inflation protection

Reinvestment Risk:
- Coupons reinvested at lower rates
- More significant in falling rate environment

Liquidity Risk:
- Some bonds difficult to sell
- Corporate bonds less liquid than Treasuries

Call Risk:
- Issuer redeems when rates fall
- Limits upside potential

Bond Investment Strategies:

Laddering:
- Buy bonds maturing at different times
- Reduces reinvestment risk
- Provides regular liquidity

Barbell:
- Short-term and long-term bonds
- Avoids intermediate maturities
- Balances yield and flexibility

Bullet:
- Bonds maturing around same date
- Used for specific future liability

Total Return:
- Active management for capital gains
- Trade based on interest rate views
- Higher risk than buy-and-hold

Bond ETFs and Funds:

Advantages:
- Diversification
- Professional management
- Liquidity
- Low minimum investment

Popular Bond ETFs:
- BND: Total US Bond Market
- TLT: 20+ Year Treasury
- LQD: Investment Grade Corporate
- HYG: High Yield Corporate
- TIP: TIPS

Considerations:
- No maturity date (perpetual)
- Daily price fluctuation
- Expense ratios reduce yield
- May not perform like individual bonds

Why Include Bonds?
- Reduce portfolio volatility
- Generate income
- Capital preservation
- Diversification from stocks
- Rebalancing opportunities
