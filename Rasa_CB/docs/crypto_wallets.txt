Cryptocurrency Wallets Guide

What is a Crypto Wallet?
A cryptocurrency wallet is a tool that allows you to store, send, and receive digital assets. Contrary to the name, wallets don't actually store your crypto - they store the private keys that give you access to your assets on the blockchain.

Types of Wallets:

1. Hot Wallets (Connected to Internet):
Software wallets that are always online

Desktop Wallets:
- Installed on your computer
- Examples: Exodus, Electrum, Atomic Wallet
- Pros: Full control, feature-rich
- Cons: Vulnerable if computer is compromised

Mobile Wallets:
- Smartphone applications
- Examples: Trust Wallet, MetaMask Mobile, Coinbase Wallet
- Pros: Convenient, portable
- Cons: Risk if phone is lost/stolen

Web Wallets:
- Accessed through browser
- Examples: MetaMask, Phantom, Rabby
- Pros: Easy access, works across devices
- Cons: Phishing risks, browser vulnerabilities

Exchange Wallets:
- Hosted by cryptocurrency exchanges
- Examples: Coinbase, Binance, Kraken
- Pros: Easy to use, integrated trading
- Cons: Not your keys, not your coins

2. Cold Wallets (Offline Storage):
Most secure option for long-term storage

Hardware Wallets:
- Physical devices that store keys offline
- Examples: Ledger Nano X/S, Trezor Model T/One, GridPlus Lattice
- Pros: Highest security, immune to online attacks
- Cons: Cost $50-250, less convenient

Paper Wallets:
- Private keys printed on paper
- Pros: Completely offline, free
- Cons: Can be damaged/lost, complex to create securely

Key Concepts:

Private Key:
- Secret code that proves ownership
- NEVER share with anyone
- If lost, funds are lost forever
- Example format: 64 hexadecimal characters

Public Key/Address:
- Like your bank account number
- Safe to share for receiving funds
- Derived from private key mathematically

Seed Phrase (Recovery Phrase):
- 12 or 24 words that can restore your wallet
- Write down and store securely offline
- NEVER store digitally or share
- Works across compatible wallets

Security Best Practices:
1. Use hardware wallets for large holdings
2. Enable two-factor authentication (2FA)
3. Store seed phrase in multiple secure locations
4. Verify addresses before sending
5. Use separate wallets for different purposes
6. Keep software updated
7. Beware of phishing sites and scams
8. Test with small amounts first

Multi-Signature Wallets:
- Require multiple keys to authorize transactions
- Great for businesses or shared accounts
- Examples: Gnosis Safe, Casa
- Adds security layer against single point of failure

Choosing a Wallet:
Consider:
- Which cryptocurrencies you need to store
- Security requirements
- Ease of use
- Backup and recovery options
- Frequency of transactions

Remember: "Not your keys, not your coins" - self-custody gives you full control but also full responsibility for security.
