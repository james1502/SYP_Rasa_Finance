# Financial Statements Explained

Financial Statements Explained

Financial statements are the report card of a company.

BEGINNER LEVEL

Income Statement: Shows revenue (sales), expenses, and profit (bottom line) over a period. Think: "How much money did the company make?"

Balance Sheet: A snapshot of what the company owns (assets) and owes (liabilities), plus shareholders' equity. Think: "What is the company worth right now?"

Cash Flow Statement: Tracks cash in and out from operations, investing, and financing. Even profitable companies can run out of cash.

INTERMEDIATE LEVEL

Revenue vs. profit: High revenue doesn't mean high profit if costs are out of control.

Operating income (EBIT): Earnings before interest and taxes, showing core profitability.

Debt-to-equity: Measures financial leverage. High levels increase risk.

Free cash flow (FCF): Cash from operations minus capital expenditures. A key measure of financial health.

Quality of earnings: Sustainable profits come from core operations, not one-time events or accounting tricks.

ADVANCED LEVEL

Accrual accounting vs. cash accounting: Understanding non-cash items like depreciation, stock-based compensation.

Red flags: Rising receivables faster than sales, inventory buildup, aggressive revenue recognition.

Segment reporting: Breaking down performance by business unit or geography.

Footnotes: Often hide critical information about debt covenants, lawsuits, or off-balance-sheet items.

Pro-forma adjustments: Companies sometimes strip out "bad" items to present a rosy picture; always compare to GAAP numbers.
