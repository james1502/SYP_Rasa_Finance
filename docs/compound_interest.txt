The Power of Compound Interest

Compound interest is the interest you earn on both your original money AND on the interest that money has already earned.

BEGINNER LEVEL

Imagine you save $100 and earn 10% interest. After year one, you have $110. In year two, you earn 10% on $110, not just $100 - that gives you $121. That $1 extra is "interest on interest." Over many years, this snowball effect turns small, regular savings into large sums.

Rule of 72: Divide 72 by your annual return to estimate how many years it takes to double your money. Example: 72 divided by 6% equals 12 years to double.

INTERMEDIATE LEVEL

Simple vs. compound interest: Simple interest is calculated only on the principal. Compound interest accelerates growth.

Compounding frequency: Daily, monthly, or yearly compounding affects total returns. More frequent compounding equals slightly higher returns.

Time horizon matters: Starting 10 years earlier can double your final nest egg, even with smaller contributions.

Real-world example: $200/month invested at 7% from age 25 becomes approximately $480,000 by age 65. Start at 35, and it's only approximately $220,000.


ADVANCED LEVEL

Continuous compounding: The mathematical limit of increasingly frequent compounding, using the formula A equals P times e to the power of rt.

Impact of taxes and inflation: Real returns after taxes and inflation reduce effective compounding, making tax-advantaged accounts like IRA and 401(k) critical.

Negative compounding (debt): Credit card debt compounds against you, often at 18-25% APR, destroying wealth faster than investments can grow.

Compounding in dividend reinvestment: Reinvesting dividends to buy more shares harnesses compound growth in equity portfolios. DRIPs automate this.

Limitations: Past returns don't guarantee future compounding; sequence-of-return risk can harm compounding in volatile markets.
