Dow Jones Industrial Average

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA), commonly known as "the Dow," is one of the oldest and most widely recognized stock market indices. It tracks 30 large, publicly-owned blue-chip companies trading on the NYSE and NASDAQ.

Key Facts:
- Created in 1896 by Charles Dow
- Contains only 30 stocks (smallest of major indices)
- Price-weighted index (higher stock prices have more influence)
- Often used as a barometer of overall U.S. stock market health

Unique Structure:
Unlike most modern indices, the Dow is price-weighted rather than market-cap weighted. This means a $300 stock has 10 times the influence of a $30 stock, regardless of company size.

The 30 Components:
Major companies include Apple, Microsoft, UnitedHealth, Goldman Sachs, Home Depot, McDonald's, Visa, Boeing, Coca-Cola, Disney, Nike, and American Express. Companies are selected by the editors of The Wall Street Journal.

Changes Over Time:
The Dow's composition changes occasionally to reflect the evolving economy. General Electric was the only original member remaining until it was removed in 2018. Recent additions include Salesforce, Amgen, and Honeywell.

Historical Significance:
The Dow was the first stock market index and remains culturally significant. Headlines often reference "the Dow" when discussing market performance, though the S&P 500 is now considered a better economic indicator.

Investment:
Popular ETFs tracking the Dow include DIA (SPDR Dow Jones Industrial Average ETF Trust).
